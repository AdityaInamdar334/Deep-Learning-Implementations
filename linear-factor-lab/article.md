# PCA Can Explain 99% of Your Data’s Variance and Still Miss What Matters

*From probabilistic PCA and independent component analysis to sparse coding and modern mechanistic interpretability: how machine learning discovers hidden features without labels.*

Imagine recording a conversation using three microphones in a room where three people are speaking simultaneously.

Every microphone captures a different mixture of the same voices. You have thousands of measurements, but none of them tells you directly what each individual person said.

There are no labels. No clean recordings. No examples showing the algorithm what the correct answer should look like. All you have is the mixed signal.

Could a machine learning algorithm discover the original sources purely from their statistical properties? More importantly, what does discovering those sources even mean mathematically?

This problem takes us into **latent representation learning**. It is the same broad scientific question that appears when researchers try to separate brain signals from electrical interference, discover features inside large language models, learn representations from unlabeled images, or understand the hidden structure of high-dimensional data.

And as we will discover, maximizing variance, minimizing reconstruction error, discovering independent components, and learning meaningful features are fundamentally different objectives. An algorithm can succeed at one while failing at another.

This article builds on Chapter 13, *Linear Factor Models*, of Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville's [Deep Learning](https://www.deeplearningbook.org/contents/linear_factors.html) (2016), connecting classical probabilistic latent variables to contemporary representation learning.

## 1. Why learning without labels is a fundamentally different problem

Throughout supervised learning, we have been concerned with learning a mapping:

**fθ: x → y**

Given sufficient examples of inputs and targets, we optimize the parameters of a model to approximate this relationship. But suppose we remove the targets. We now have a dataset of observations x₁, x₂, …, xₙ.

What should the model learn?

There is no single correct answer. We might want to estimate the probability of an observation, generate new examples, reconstruct missing information, identify independent sources, or discover features that explain the underlying structure.

Rather than directly modeling a target, we can attempt to model the distribution of the observations themselves, pθ(x). One particularly powerful approach introduces hidden variables:

**pθ(x) = ∫ pθ(x | h) p(h) dh**

Here, h represents latent factors that are not directly observed. For example, an image of a car might depend on its shape, color, orientation, lighting conditions, and position. These factors collectively influence the pixels we observe.

The pixels are measurements. The latent variables are an attempt to represent the structure behind those measurements.

**[Insert Figure 1: latent-factors.png]**

*Figure 1. Illustrative latent factors and the observations they generate. An unsupervised model does not automatically recover these semantic concepts.*

We are no longer simply asking what the correct output is. We are asking what underlying structure could have produced the observations.

## 2. The simplest possible generative model

Before constructing a variational autoencoder or diffusion model, consider a much simpler possibility. Suppose every observation is generated by combining a collection of hidden factors linearly and adding noise.

**x = Wh + b + ε**

- x is the observed D-dimensional vector.
- h is the K-dimensional latent representation.
- W is the D × K decoder matrix.
- b is the mean or bias vector.
- ε represents observation noise.

Typically, we begin with a factorized prior, p(h) = ∏ᵢ p(hᵢ). Each column of W represents a direction or feature in observation space.

**[Insert Figure 2: linear-model.png]**

*Figure 2. A linear factor model combines independent latent variables through a mixing matrix and adds observation noise.*

In NumPy, the generative process is remarkably simple:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 1000
latent_dim = 2
observed_dim = 5

h = rng.normal(size=(n_samples, latent_dim))
W = rng.normal(size=(observed_dim, latent_dim))
noise = 0.1 * rng.normal(size=(n_samples, observed_dim))
X = h @ W.T + noise
print(X.shape)
```

Expected output: (1000, 5).

The interesting problem is reversing this process. Given only X, can we recover the underlying factors?

The answer depends on the assumptions we make about those factors, the noise, and the objective we optimize. That is precisely what distinguishes PCA, probabilistic PCA, factor analysis, ICA, and sparse coding.

## 3. Principal component analysis: discovering directions of variation

PCA has a history stretching back to Pearson (1901) and Hotelling (1933), long before modern neural networks existed. Its central observation is that high-dimensional measurements often contain redundant information.

Suppose we have thousands of observations with 100 features. If those features are strongly correlated, much of the variation might be captured by a considerably smaller number of directions. PCA finds those directions.

Given centered data, its covariance matrix is:

**C = XᵀX / N**

We compute the eigendecomposition:

**Cvᵢ = λᵢvᵢ**

The eigenvectors describe directions in observation space. The eigenvalues describe the amount of variance associated with those directions. We select the eigenvectors with the largest eigenvalues.

**[Insert Figure 3: pca-projection.png]**

*Figure 3. PCA minimizes squared orthogonal reconstruction error, equivalently maximizing projected variance. Illustrative data.*

### Implementing PCA from scratch

```python
def fit_pca(X, n_components):
    mean = X.mean(axis=0)
    X_centered = X - mean
    covariance = X_centered.T @ X_centered / len(X_centered)
    eigenvalues, eigenvectors = np.linalg.eigh(covariance)
    indices = np.argsort(eigenvalues)[::-1]
    components = eigenvectors[:, indices[:n_components]]
    return mean, components, eigenvalues[indices]


def encode(X, mean, components):
    return (X - mean) @ components


def decode(H, mean, components):
    return H @ components.T + mean
```

The reconstruction is x̂ = WWᵀ(x − μ) + μ, where W contains the orthonormal principal directions.

When we retain K principal components, the minimum expected squared reconstruction error equals the sum of discarded eigenvalues, λₖ₊₁ through λᴅ.

Under the squared-error and linear-subspace assumptions, we know exactly what information is lost in terms of variance. But we do not necessarily know whether the discarded information matters.

### When variance is the wrong objective

Imagine two independent features: a nuisance variable with variance 100 and a perfectly predictive binary feature with variance 1.

The first principal component captures 100 / 101 ≈ 99.01% of the total variance. Yet that component contains no information about the target if the target depends exclusively on the second feature.

A classifier using only the retained component cannot outperform chance in the population for balanced labels, despite PCA preserving approximately 99% of the variance.

**[Insert Figure 4: variance-counterexample.png]**

*Figure 4. Analytical counterexample: the nuisance variable contains 99.01% of the variance, while the predictive feature contains only 0.99%. This is not a measured training result.*

This reveals an important limitation of unsupervised learning: an objective can be optimized perfectly while ignoring the information needed by a downstream application.

## 4. Probabilistic PCA: what if PCA could generate data?

Ordinary PCA defines a projection and reconstruction mechanism. It does not, by itself, specify a normalized probability distribution over observations.

Tipping and Bishop (1999) addressed this limitation by formulating PCA as a latent-variable probability model. They assume standard Gaussian latent factors and isotropic Gaussian noise:

**h ∼ N(0, I)**

**ε ∼ N(0, σ²I)**

The observation model becomes x | h ∼ N(Wh + b, σ²I). Because a linear transformation of Gaussian variables remains Gaussian, we can analytically integrate out the latent variables:

**x ∼ N(b, WWᵀ + σ²I)**

This is probabilistic PCA. We now have a distribution from which we can sample and with which we can evaluate the likelihood of observations.

### Deriving the covariance

Starting with x − b = Wh + ε and assuming independence between h and ε:

**Cov(x) = W Cov(h) Wᵀ + Cov(ε) = WWᵀ + σ²I**

We have decomposed the observed covariance into structured latent variation and isotropic observation noise.

**[Insert Figure 5: ppca.png]**

*Figure 5. Probability concentration around a lower-dimensional linear manifold.*

### Maximum likelihood solution

Let U contain the leading K eigenvectors of the sample covariance, and Λ contain their eigenvalues on the diagonal. A maximum likelihood solution is:

**W = U (Λ − σ²I)½ R**

Here R is an arbitrary orthogonal matrix and the exponent denotes a matrix square root. The maximum likelihood noise estimate is the mean of the D − K discarded eigenvalues.

```python
def fit_ppca(X, k):
    mean = X.mean(axis=0)
    Xc = X - mean
    C = Xc.T @ Xc / len(Xc)
    values, vectors = np.linalg.eigh(C)
    order = np.argsort(values)[::-1]
    values = values[order]
    vectors = vectors[:, order]
    sigma2 = values[k:].mean()
    W = vectors[:, :k] * np.sqrt(
        np.maximum(values[:k] - sigma2, 0)
    )
    return mean, W, sigma2
```

This gives us a simple probabilistic generative model without requiring neural network training.

### Posterior inference

Given a new observation, define M = WᵀW + σ²I. The posterior distribution is Gaussian, with mean and covariance:

**Posterior mean = M⁻¹Wᵀ(x − b)**

**Posterior covariance = σ²M⁻¹**

We obtain not only an estimate of the hidden factors, but also their posterior uncertainty.

```python
def infer_ppca(X, mean, W, sigma2):
    k = W.shape[1]
    M = W.T @ W + sigma2 * np.eye(k)
    posterior_mean = np.linalg.solve(
        M, W.T @ (X - mean).T
    ).T
    posterior_covariance = sigma2 * np.linalg.solve(
        M, np.eye(k)
    )
    return posterior_mean, posterior_covariance
```

This is one of the conceptual foundations of modern probabilistic representation learning. Variational autoencoders later address a related problem using nonlinear neural networks and approximate posterior inference.

## 5. Factor analysis: not every measurement is equally reliable

PPCA assumes that all observed dimensions share the same noise variance. In real systems, that assumption is frequently unrealistic.

Consider a network of sensors. Some sensors may be extremely accurate, while others produce noisy measurements. Treating all of them as equally reliable can distort the learned representation.

Factor analysis relaxes this assumption. The latent prior remains h ∼ N(0, I), but noise follows ε ∼ N(0, Ψ), where Ψ is a diagonal matrix with a separate variance ψᵢ for each observed feature.

**C = WWᵀ + Ψ**

**[Insert Figure 6: noise-covariance.png]**

*Figure 6. PPCA assumes equal noise variances; factor analysis allows different variances on the diagonal.*

```python
from sklearn.decomposition import FactorAnalysis

model = FactorAnalysis(n_components=2, random_state=42)
H = model.fit_transform(X)
print("Latent representations:", H.shape)
print("Noise variances:", model.noise_variance_)
```

The model estimates shared latent variation separately from dimension-specific noise. However, the underlying Gaussian latent space introduces a deeper problem. What if the factors we want to discover are not merely uncorrelated, but statistically independent?

## 6. Independent component analysis: when PCA cannot separate signals

Return to our original cocktail-party problem. Suppose we have two independent source signals collected in a vector s. The microphones observe x = As, where A is an unknown mixing matrix. We want to estimate an unmixing matrix B so that ŝ = Bx.

PCA can produce uncorrelated components, but uncorrelated variables are not necessarily independent.

For example, let u ∼ N(0, 1) and v = u² − 1. Their covariance is zero because E[uv] = E[u³ − u] = 0. Yet v is completely determined by u.

**[Insert Figure 7: dependent-uncorrelated.png]**

*Figure 7. A deterministic quadratic relationship can have zero population covariance.*

ICA attempts to identify components whose joint distribution factorizes: p(s₁, …, sₖ) = ∏ᵢ p(sᵢ).

### Why ICA requires non-Gaussian information

Suppose s ∼ N(0, I). For any orthogonal matrix R, Rs has exactly the same standard Gaussian distribution. We cannot uniquely identify the original orientation from the observed distribution alone.

Classical linear ICA resolves this ambiguity using non-Gaussian source distributions, under assumptions including an invertible mixing process and at most one Gaussian component. Even then, the recovered sources remain ambiguous up to scaling and permutation (Comon, 1994; Hyvärinen and Oja, 2000).

### Building the signal-separation experiment

```python
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA, FastICA

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 5000
sources = np.column_stack([
    rng.laplace(size=n_samples),
    rng.uniform(-2, 2, size=n_samples)
])
sources -= sources.mean(axis=0)
sources /= sources.std(axis=0)
A = np.array([[1.0, 0.8], [0.4, 1.2]])
observations = sources @ A.T
pca_sources = PCA(n_components=2).fit_transform(observations)
ica_sources = FastICA(
    n_components=2, whiten="unit-variance",
    random_state=42, max_iter=2000
).fit_transform(observations)
```

The correct evaluation should account for sign changes and permutation ambiguity.

```python
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment


def source_recovery_score(true, estimated):
    k = true.shape[1]
    correlations = np.abs(np.corrcoef(
        true.T, estimated.T
    )[:k, k:])
    rows, cols = linear_sum_assignment(-correlations)
    return correlations[rows, cols].mean()
```

A high score indicates that each recovered component closely resembles one of the original sources, after accounting for ordering and sign.

**[Insert Figure 8: source-separation.png]**

*Figure 8. Conceptual blind source separation. The illustration is not a measured ICA result.*

The objective is not simply to compress the observations. It is to discover the independent processes responsible for producing them.

## 7. Slow feature analysis: learning from time rather than labels

Imagine a camera watching a moving object. Individual pixels can change dramatically between consecutive frames, even though the identity of the object remains constant.

Rather than maximizing variance or statistical independence, what if we search for features that change slowly over time? Wiskott and Sejnowski (2002) formalized this idea through slow feature analysis (SFA).

Given a feature extractor yₜ = f(xₜ), we minimize the expected squared difference between consecutive outputs:

**Lslow = E[(yₜ₊₁ − yₜ)²]**

But a model that always outputs zero achieves a perfect slowness loss. To avoid this trivial solution, SFA imposes zero mean, unit variance, and pairwise decorrelation. These constraints prevent constant features and prevent all features from representing the same slow signal.

**[Insert Figure 9: slow-features.png]**

*Figure 9. Illustrative temporal structure: rapidly changing measurements can contain slowly evolving underlying factors.*

### Solving SFA using linear algebra

For a linear feature extractor yₜ = wᵀxₜ, the slowness objective becomes wᵀCΔw, where CΔ is the second-moment matrix of consecutive differences. The unit-variance constraint is wᵀCₓw = 1.

This results in a generalized eigenvalue problem:

**CΔw = λCₓw**

Unlike PCA, where we select the largest eigenvalues, SFA selects the smallest eigenvalues of this problem.

```python
from scipy.linalg import eigh


def fit_sfa(X, k):
    mean = X.mean(axis=0)
    Xc = X - mean
    dX = np.diff(Xc, axis=0)
    Cx = Xc.T @ Xc / len(Xc)
    Cd = dX.T @ dX / len(dX)
    Cx += 1e-8 * np.eye(X.shape[1])
    values, vectors = eigh(Cd, Cx)
    return mean, vectors[:, :k], values[:k]
```

There is no backpropagation required for this linear formulation. The solution follows directly from the mathematical constraints. A nonlinear basis expansion, such as quadratic features, can extend SFA beyond linear functions.

### The limitation of slowness

A feature can change quickly and still be useful. Consider a robot tracking a fast-moving object. Suppressing its position representation because the object moves quickly would be counterproductive.

**Predictability may be more useful than slowness alone.** A moving object can change position rapidly while remaining predictable under an appropriate dynamical model. This is a conceptual connection between classical temporal representation learning and modern predictive representations and world models.

## 8. Sparse coding: discovering features that are rarely active together

Sparse coding begins with a different hypothesis. Rather than assuming that every latent feature contributes to every observation, suppose an observation can be explained using only a small subset of available features.

A handwritten digit, for example, might be represented through strokes, edges, curves, and intersections. Most possible strokes are irrelevant to a particular image.

Olshausen and Field's influential 1996 work demonstrated how sparsity objectives could produce localized, oriented image features resembling properties of biological visual receptive fields.

**L(h) = ½ ‖x − Dh‖² + λ ‖h‖₁**

Here D is a dictionary. The first term encourages accurate reconstruction. The second encourages a representation in which most activations are zero. Under Gaussian observation noise and a Laplace prior, this objective also has a maximum-a-posteriori interpretation.

**[Insert Figure 10: sparse-dictionary.png]**

*Figure 10. An illustrative dictionary in which only three of eight features contribute to a reconstruction.*

### Why the L1 penalty produces sparsity

Consider the simplest possible sparse coding problem: minimize ½(h − x)² + λ|h|. Its solution is the soft-thresholding operator:

**h* = sign(x) max(|x| − λ, 0)**

**[Insert Figure 11: soft-threshold.png]**

*Figure 11. Soft thresholding sets small coefficients exactly to zero and shrinks larger coefficients.*

Unlike an L2 penalty, which generally shrinks coefficients continuously, L1 regularization can produce exact zeros. This is the computational mechanism behind many sparse representation algorithms.

## 9. Implementing sparse coding from scratch with ISTA

For a general dictionary, we cannot solve the sparse coding problem through a single soft-thresholding operation. Instead, we alternate between a gradient step on the reconstruction objective and a proximal step enforcing sparsity.

This algorithm is known as iterative shrinkage-thresholding, or ISTA. Its update is:

**hₜ₊₁ = S(hₜ − ηDᵀ(Dhₜ − x), ηλ)**

Here S is soft thresholding, η is the step size, and λ controls sparsity.

```python
def soft_threshold(x, threshold):
    return np.sign(x) * np.maximum(np.abs(x) - threshold, 0.0)


def ista(X, D, lam=0.1, steps=200):
    n_samples = len(X)
    n_features = D.shape[1]
    H = np.zeros((n_samples, n_features))
    lipschitz = np.linalg.norm(D.T @ D, ord=2)
    step_size = 1.0 / lipschitz
    for _ in range(steps):
        residual = H @ D.T - X
        gradient = residual @ D
        H = soft_threshold(
            H - step_size * gradient, step_size * lam
        )
    return H
```

We can construct a synthetic sparse representation problem.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
D = rng.normal(size=(16, 32))
D /= np.linalg.norm(D, axis=0, keepdims=True)
H_true = np.zeros((1000, 32))
for i in range(len(H_true)):
    active = rng.choice(32, size=3, replace=False)
    H_true[i, active] = rng.normal(size=3)
X_sparse = H_true @ D.T + 0.01 * rng.normal(size=(1000, 16))
H_est = ista(X_sparse, D, lam=0.1)
X_reconstructed = H_est @ D.T
mse = np.mean((X_sparse - X_reconstructed) ** 2)
sparsity = np.mean(np.abs(H_est) < 1e-6)
print("Reconstruction MSE:", mse)
print("Fraction of zero coefficients:", sparsity)
```

The dictionary contains 32 features in a 16-dimensional observation space. This is an **overcomplete representation**.

Unlike PCA, sparse coding does not necessarily seek to reduce the dimensionality of the representation. It may increase the number of available representational directions while restricting how many are active for any particular observation.

The example uses a known dictionary to isolate sparse inference. Learning a dictionary also requires optimizing D, typically alternating between estimating codes and updating dictionary atoms under a norm constraint. Without that constraint, scaling the dictionary up and the codes down can artificially reduce the sparsity penalty.

That distinction is crucial to understanding why sparse coding has become relevant again in modern AI research.

## 10. From sparse coding to sparse autoencoders inside LLMs

A large language model might have a hidden representation with thousands of dimensions. Researchers would like to determine what information those dimensions encode.

The difficulty is that individual neurons do not necessarily correspond to individual human-interpretable concepts. Different features may be represented through overlapping combinations of neurons, a phenomenon commonly discussed as feature superposition (Elhage et al., 2022).

Sparse autoencoders attempt to discover a potentially much larger collection of features from these activations (Bricken et al., 2023; Templeton et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024).

**[Insert Figure 12: sparse-autoencoder.png]**

*Figure 12. A sparse autoencoder expands a dense activation into an overcomplete sparse representation and reconstructs the original activation.*

A simple sparse autoencoder objective is:

**h = ReLU(Wₑx + bₑ)**

**x̂ = Wᴅh + bᴅ**

**L = ‖x − x̂‖² + λ ‖h‖₁**

The resemblance to classical sparse coding is not accidental. The primary difference is how we infer the sparse representation. Classical sparse coding uses an optimization procedure such as ISTA for each input. A sparse autoencoder learns a parameterized encoder that approximates the inference process.

### PyTorch implementation

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F


class SparseAutoencoder(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, input_dim, feature_dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.encoder = nn.Linear(input_dim, feature_dim)
        self.decoder = nn.Linear(feature_dim, input_dim)

    def forward(self, x):
        features = F.relu(self.encoder(x))
        reconstruction = self.decoder(features)
        return reconstruction, features


def sae_loss(model, x, sparsity_weight):
    reconstruction, features = model(x)
    reconstruction_loss = F.mse_loss(reconstruction, x)
    sparsity_loss = features.abs().mean()
    return reconstruction_loss + sparsity_weight * sparsity_loss
```

This is a minimal educational SAE. Practical implementations typically require additional mechanisms, such as decoder normalization, dead-feature monitoring, activation preprocessing, and carefully chosen sparsity objectives.

Recent research continues to investigate the relationship between neural superposition and sparse interpretable codes. However, we should not confuse discovering a sparse basis with proving that every discovered feature has a unique semantic meaning or causal role. Those are different scientific claims.

## 11. The problem that connects all these methods: identifiability

Suppose we train a latent-variable model and obtain excellent reconstruction performance. Have we discovered the true hidden factors? Not necessarily.

Consider x = Wh. Let R be an invertible matrix. We can rewrite the same observation as:

**x = (WR⁻¹)(Rh)**

Define W′ = WR⁻¹ and h′ = Rh. Then x = W′h′. The reconstructed observation remains exactly the same.

This means infinitely many representations can produce identical reconstructions. Without additional assumptions, reconstruction alone cannot tell us which representation corresponds to the true underlying factors.

**[Insert Figure 13: identifiability.png]**

*Figure 13. Changing latent coordinates and compensating in the decoder preserves the observation. A fixed probabilistic prior can restrict the allowed transformations.*

Each linear factor model addresses this ambiguity by introducing different assumptions. PCA imposes orthogonality and orders features by variance. ICA assumes statistical independence and non-Gaussianity. SFA exploits temporal behavior. Sparse coding encourages representations with few active features. Factor analysis models shared variation separately from observation-specific noise.

None of these assumptions is universally correct. The challenge is choosing an inductive bias that matches the data-generating process.

### Modern nonlinear ICA

Extending ICA to nonlinear transformations makes the identifiability problem considerably more difficult.

Khemakhem et al. (2020) demonstrated that certain nonlinear latent-variable models can become identifiable under additional assumptions involving auxiliary variables and conditional latent distributions. Subsequent research explored whether sparse mechanisms and multiple views could provide additional structure for recovering hidden variables (Gresele et al., 2020; Lachapelle et al., 2022).

The 2026 preprint *Statistical and Structural Identifiability in Representation Learning* further investigates the relationship between representation identifiability and structural disentanglement. See the reference section for the source listed in the original draft.

The message is consistent across these developments: meaningful factor recovery requires assumptions beyond merely fitting the observed data.

## 12. Choosing the right representation-learning objective

**PCA:** preserve variance under a linear low-dimensional approximation.

**Probabilistic PCA:** model data likelihood under isotropic Gaussian noise.

**Factor analysis:** discover shared latent variation with diagonal observation noise.

**ICA:** recover independent factors under non-Gaussian source assumptions.

**SFA:** learn slowly changing features using temporal structure.

**Sparse coding:** reconstruct observations using few active features.

**Sparse autoencoder:** learn efficient sparse inference using a feature dictionary.

These methods are not competing answers to exactly the same problem. They describe different interpretations of what constitutes a useful representation.

A representation suitable for compression may not be suitable for source separation. A representation suitable for reconstruction may not be interpretable. A representation suitable for interpretation may not preserve every detail needed for prediction.

Understanding those differences is more useful than memorizing the algorithms individually.

## 13. Why linear factor models still matter

Modern machine learning architectures are considerably more sophisticated than the linear models we have studied. But many of their underlying principles are recognizable.

- PCA and linear autoencoders share the goal of low-dimensional reconstruction.
- Probabilistic PCA and variational autoencoders share the problem of probabilistic latent inference.
- ICA and nonlinear disentanglement investigate independent and identifiable factors.
- Slow feature analysis and predictive representation learning exploit temporal structure as supervision.
- Sparse coding and sparse autoencoders use overcomplete feature dictionaries.

These are conceptual connections, not claims that each modern method is a direct historical descendant of only one classical technique.

The underlying scientific question remains surprisingly consistent: how can we learn representations that expose the structure of observations without requiring humans to label every relevant factor?

## 14. Conclusion

Principal component analysis initially appears to be primarily a dimensionality reduction algorithm. But understanding PCA as a linear factor model reveals a considerably deeper connection.

PCA, ICA, factor analysis, slow feature analysis, and sparse coding are all different attempts to infer hidden structure from observations. They disagree because they make different assumptions about what that structure should look like.

PCA prioritizes variation. ICA prioritizes independence. SFA prioritizes temporal stability. Sparse coding prioritizes selective feature activation. Probabilistic factor models prioritize explaining the observed distribution.

Modern deep learning extends these ideas using nonlinear architectures, larger datasets, more sophisticated probability models, and increasingly powerful computational infrastructure.

But the central question has not disappeared. A neural network can reconstruct its inputs, minimize its loss, and achieve excellent benchmark performance without necessarily learning the representation we hoped it would discover.

Learning a representation is easy to describe. Understanding what that representation actually means remains one of the most interesting problems in machine learning research.
