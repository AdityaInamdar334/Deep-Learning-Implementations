He Discovered Superconductivity. The Same Experiment Hid Another Quantum Revolution.
A Dutch physicist cooled liquid helium below a phase transition in 1908 and spent the next 18 years looking at a superfluid without recognizing it. The explanation took another 20 years, and now runs the Large Hadron Collider.

You can produce a new phase of matter without being able to recognize it. On July 10, 1908, Heike Kamerlingh Onnes cooled liquid helium to approximately 1.7 Kelvin, below the 2.17 K lambda point, creating a superfluid without knowing that superfluidity existed as a concept. The reason it took another 30 years to name is not a failure of observation but a limit of theory, and the physics behind it now cools the LHC, powers tabletop black-hole analogues, and reframes how we think about seeing something the current paradigm says cannot exist.
On April 8, 1911, a Dutch physicist recorded one of the strangest observations in the history of experimental science.
Heike Kamerlingh Onnes was working in his cryogenic laboratory at Leiden University, cooling mercury to temperatures barely above absolute zero. The experiment was meant to answer a straightforward question: what happens to the electrical resistance of a metal when almost all its thermal energy has been removed?
For years, physicists had debated the possibilities. Perhaps the resistance would continue decreasing. Perhaps impurities would impose a final limit. Perhaps the electrons would become immobile, causing resistance to increase.
Onnes was about to discover something nobody had anticipated.
At approximately four Kelvin, mercury’s electrical resistance collapsed to a value his instruments could no longer measure. His notebook recorded the result as practically zero.
Superconductivity had been discovered. It was one of the greatest experimental breakthroughs of the twentieth century.
But something else happened that day. As the researchers continued cooling their liquid helium, the boiling suddenly stopped. The liquid appeared unusually calm, even though it was still evaporating. Onnes noticed it and wrote it down.
He did not know that he had just crossed another quantum phase transition. One that would eventually reveal a liquid capable of flowing through tiny channels with almost no resistance, creeping over the walls of containers, transporting heat in extraordinary ways, and supporting vortices whose circulation is constrained by quantum mechanics.
That second phenomenon was superfluidity.
The extraordinary part is not that Onnes lacked the equipment to observe it. He had already created the required temperatures. In fact, three years earlier, on July 10, 1908, his first successful liquid-helium experiment had briefly reached approximately 1.7 Kelvin, well below the superfluid transition. The necessary physical state had already existed in his apparatus.
But recognizing a new phase of matter requires more than producing it. It requires knowing which observations are anomalies, which measurements are trustworthy, and which theoretical assumptions might be wrong.
The decisive experiments establishing superfluidity were not published until January 1938, almost thirty years after helium was first liquefied and twelve years after Onnes’s death.
That makes this story more than a history of low-temperature physics. It is a story about how scientific understanding develops when experimental observations arrive before the concepts needed to interpret them. And the physics itself is even stranger than the history.

1. The night liquid helium stopped boiling
Imagine looking through a laboratory window into a container of liquid helium.
At its normal boiling point, approximately 4.22 Kelvin at atmospheric pressure, the liquid is exceptionally cold. That’s about minus 269 degrees Celsius. Even a small amount of heat entering the container can cause helium to vaporize. The resulting bubbles make the liquid look restless.
Now reduce the pressure above the liquid. As helium evaporates, it carries energy away. The remaining liquid becomes colder. This is the same broad principle used in evaporative cooling, although the cryogenic apparatus operates under conditions far removed from ordinary experience.
Continue cooling toward approximately 2.17 Kelvin. The character of the liquid changes. Under conditions where bubbling previously occurred, the visible boiling can subside dramatically. The surface becomes unusually calm.
Yet the helium has not stopped evaporating. What has changed is its ability to redistribute heat.
Below this temperature, helium-4 enters a phase called helium II. Above it, liquid helium-4 is called helium I. The boundary is known as the lambda transition. The precise lambda temperature depends on pressure. At saturated vapor pressure, the accepted value is approximately 2.1768 Kelvin.
The name comes from the peculiar shape of the specific-heat curve near the transition, which resembles the Greek letter lambda.
In modern terms, this transition marks the emergence of superfluid behavior. But in 1911, there was no developed theory of superfluid helium. There was no established two-fluid hydrodynamics. The relationship between Bose-Einstein condensation and liquid helium had not yet been formulated. And the quantum mechanics required to understand the phenomenon was still being developed.
To Onnes, the suddenly calm liquid was an unusual observation during an already remarkable experiment. The mercury had just become superconducting. That was the discovery that captured his attention. The helium was providing the cooling. Its own unusual behavior was not yet the focus.
This is an important distinction. The modern meaning of an observation may be clear to us because we already know the theory. That does not mean the same interpretation was available to the person who first recorded it [1][2].
2. The race to make the coldest liquid on Earth
The path to superfluidity began long before quantum mechanics.
Throughout the nineteenth century, experimental physicists competed to reach lower temperatures. They learned to liquefy gases that had once been considered impossible to condense.
The challenge was partly theoretical. A gas does not necessarily liquefy when compressed. Above its critical temperature, compression alone cannot create a distinct liquid phase. The gas must first be cooled enough for intermolecular attractions to produce condensation.
The challenge was also technological. Researchers needed pumps, pressure vessels, insulation, refrigeration cycles, and increasingly pure gases.
Early progress came from scientists such as Michael Faraday, who liquefied several gases during the 1820s and later investigated substances that resisted conventional compression. The development of better cooling methods gradually brought oxygen, nitrogen, and hydrogen into the liquid state. By 1898, James Dewar had successfully liquefied hydrogen. Its normal boiling point is around 20 Kelvin.
That was an extraordinary achievement. Only helium remained beyond reach.
Why helium was so difficult
Helium is a noble gas. Its electrons form a tightly bound configuration. Individual helium atoms interact only weakly through dispersion forces, often called London dispersion forces. These arise from quantum fluctuations and correlations in their electron distributions.
The attractions are real. But they are very weak. That means helium requires exceptionally low temperatures before a liquid phase becomes stable.
There was another problem. Helium was scarce in terrestrial laboratories. Although it is abundant in the universe, early researchers had limited access to purified samples on Earth. Helium can accumulate in certain minerals through radioactive alpha decay. An alpha particle is a helium nucleus. Once it acquires electrons, it becomes an ordinary helium atom.
Onnes obtained helium from mineral sources and invested considerable resources in purifying it. This was not a simple experiment conducted by one physicist at a small bench. It required an entire low-temperature laboratory.
Technicians maintained compressors. Glassblowers constructed delicate vessels. Chemists purified gas samples. Instrument makers developed specialized components. Onnes was building what was effectively an early industrial-scale research facility.
That organizational achievement was as important to the discovery as the thermodynamic theory [1].
The cooling technique
Onnes used a cascade of refrigeration stages.
Liquid air provided initial cooling. Liquid hydrogen brought the helium system much closer to the required temperature. Then the helium itself was circulated through a regenerative cooling system.
A key mechanism was Joule-Thomson expansion. Under suitable conditions, a real gas can cool when it expands through a restriction. The effect depends on the gas, its initial temperature, and its thermodynamic properties. Helium is particularly demanding because it must first be precooled below its inversion temperature for the expansion to produce the desired cooling.
Onnes combined this effect with recirculation. Cold returning helium helped cool incoming helium. Each cycle brought the system closer to liquefaction.
The apparatus was complicated. And the available helium supply was too valuable to waste.
3. July 10, 1908: sixty milliliters that changed physics
On July 10, 1908, the Leiden laboratory began one of its most ambitious experimental runs.
The team had accumulated approximately 75 liters of liquid air. By early afternoon, it had produced about 20 liters of liquid hydrogen. The hydrogen was needed to precool the helium apparatus.
The researchers then began circulating compressed helium through the system. The gas pressure reached approximately 100 atmospheres. The temperature gradually fell. Six Kelvin. Then lower.
But the thermometer eventually appeared to stabilize. There was no dramatic visual transformation. Helium is nearly transparent. Its refractive index in the liquid state is very close to that of the gas. A small quantity of liquid helium can be difficult to distinguish from its surroundings.
One observer suggested that the unusually stable thermometer might already be immersed in a liquid bath. Onnes examined the apparatus with light directed at an angle. A liquid surface became visible. In his report, he described its boundary as sharply defined.
The team had succeeded. They produced approximately 60 milliliters of liquid helium. Enough to fill a small cup. That was all. But it was the first time helium had been reliably liquefied.
The achievement gave physicists access to a temperature range that had previously been inaccessible. And it made further discoveries possible.
The detail usually missing from the story
The experiment did not simply stop at helium’s normal boiling temperature. Onnes also reduced the vapor pressure and cooled the liquid further.
According to historian Dirk van Delft’s reconstruction, the apparatus reached approximately 1.7 Kelvin. That is below the helium-4 lambda transition.
So the statement that Onnes first produced superfluid helium in 1908 is defensible. But it needs careful wording. He produced helium under conditions we now identify as superfluid. He did not identify superfluidity as a distinct physical phenomenon.
He was attempting to liquefy helium and investigate whether it could freeze. The superfluid phase was an unrecognized consequence of reaching those temperatures.
He also discovered that helium would not readily solidify simply by cooling it. That observation would later become another important clue about its quantum nature.
Onnes received the 1913 Nobel Prize in Physics for his low-temperature investigations, including the production of liquid helium. Not for discovering superfluidity. That recognition came much later [1][3].
4. Two quantum transitions in one laboratory
Three years after liquefying helium, Onnes used it to investigate electrical resistance.
The experiment of April 8, 1911, produced the first clear evidence of superconductivity in mercury.
At ordinary temperatures, metals resist electrical current. Their electrons scatter from lattice vibrations, impurities, and other disturbances. Cooling reduces some of these scattering mechanisms.
But Onnes observed something more dramatic than a smooth reduction. Mercury’s resistance fell abruptly below the sensitivity of his apparatus. Later measurements established a sharp transition near 4.2 Kelvin.
Today, we understand conventional superconductivity through the formation of correlated electron pairs and a macroscopic quantum state. But that explanation would not arrive for another four decades. Onnes had discovered the phenomenon before its microscopic theory existed.
Then, during the same day’s work, the researchers cooled the liquid helium further. His notebook records a remarkable change. The visible boiling suddenly stopped as the helium approached the lowest temperatures reached in the experiment.
Historian Dirk van Delft and physicist Peter Kes examined the original laboratory notebooks and reconstructed this sequence in Physics Today [2]. Their account establishes something extraordinary. The Leiden team observed evidence of two different quantum phase transitions in the same experimental program. One was superconductivity. The other was the transition into helium II. Only one was recognized at the time.
Why didn’t Onnes immediately investigate?
It is tempting to suggest that he simply ignored an obvious discovery. But that would impose modern knowledge on an earlier experiment.
Scientists then lacked a framework for understanding a liquid that could transport heat and mass in radically different ways below a critical temperature. Even the existence of distinct helium I and helium II phases was not yet established.
The observation could be recorded as unusual boiling behavior without revealing a new quantum state. And the laboratory’s immediate purpose was to study metals. The helium was the refrigerant. Its behavior mattered operationally, but the new electrical result was the urgent scientific finding.
This does not make the missed transition insignificant. It makes the history more interesting. The same apparatus that revealed superconductivity was already revealing the limits of the physics used to interpret it.
5. The clues between 1911 and 1938
Superfluidity was not discovered in one isolated moment after decades of complete silence. The evidence accumulated gradually.
In 1922, Onnes observed that liquid helium in connected or concentric vessels could equalize its levels in an unexpectedly rapid way. At the time, the behavior could be interpreted through evaporation and condensation processes. But later researchers recognized that it was connected to the strange transport properties of helium II.
By the 1920s, careful measurements revealed additional anomalies. The density of liquid helium exhibited unusual behavior near the temperature now associated with the lambda transition. The specific heat displayed a pronounced anomaly.
In 1927, Willem Keesom and Mieczysław Wolfke argued that liquid helium existed in two distinct phases separated by a transition around 2.2 Kelvin. The phases became known as helium I and helium II [4].
By then, Onnes had died. But the mystery continued.
Why the specific heat mattered
Specific heat measures how much energy is required to raise a substance’s temperature. Near a phase transition, the way a system stores energy can change dramatically.
For helium-4, the specific-heat anomaly near the lambda temperature is especially distinctive. The curve has a sharp, lambda-like shape. In modern statistical physics, this is evidence of critical behavior associated with a continuous phase transition.
The liquid does not simply become a less viscous version of its former self. Its collective thermodynamic state changes. That distinction was essential.
An ordinary liquid becoming progressively less viscous is one phenomenon. A new phase emerging at a sharply defined temperature is another. The latter required a different theoretical explanation.
1938: a new experimental question
The decisive shift came when physicists stopped asking only about helium’s thermodynamic properties. They began measuring how the liquid actually flowed. That changed the interpretation of the entire problem.
6. The experiment that finally established superfluidity
In the late 1930s, two research groups investigated the flow resistance of helium II.
Pyotr Kapitsa was working in Moscow. John F. Allen and A. Don Misener were working in Cambridge. Their experimental approaches differed. But both found that helium II behaved in ways conventional fluid mechanics could not explain.
Kapitsa’s experiment
Kapitsa studied helium moving through an exceptionally narrow gap. The observed resistance to flow was extraordinarily small. He inferred that the liquid’s effective viscosity was vastly lower than ordinary fluid behavior would predict.
It was natural to compare the phenomenon with superconductivity. In a superconductor, electrical current can persist without ordinary resistance. Here was a liquid apparently capable of moving without ordinary viscous resistance. Kapitsa proposed the name superfluid [5].
Allen and Misener’s experiment
Allen and Misener used narrow capillaries. They examined the relationship between pressure difference and the resulting flow.
In an ordinary viscous liquid, forcing fluid through a thinner tube strongly changes the flow resistance. The geometry matters enormously. But helium II did not obey the expected relationships. Its flow could become almost independent of the applied pressure difference.
The researchers concluded that the observed motion could not be meaningfully described using conventional laminar or turbulent-flow formulas [6].
This was a stronger result than merely finding a small viscosity coefficient. The entire character of the flow was unusual.
Two papers in the same issue
On January 8, 1938, Nature published the two reports consecutively. Kapitsa’s letter appeared on page 74. Allen and Misener’s appeared on page 75.
Both established extraordinary flow behavior below the lambda point. The experiments were performed independently. The physical interpretation was now unavoidable.
The liquid was not simply behaving like an ordinary fluid with a very small friction coefficient. It had entered a state requiring a new description of fluid motion. Superfluidity had become an experimental fact.
Kapitsa later received a share of the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics for his fundamental work in low-temperature physics. Allen and Misener did not receive the Nobel Prize, although their contribution is essential to the history [4][5][6].
7. Why liquid helium behaves like two fluids at once
The next question was obvious. How can one liquid behave as though it has no viscosity in one experiment, while still displaying viscous behavior in another?
The answer required a new way of describing fluid motion.
In 1938, László Tisza proposed an influential two-fluid interpretation. Lev Landau later developed a more complete hydrodynamic and excitation-based theory in 1941 [7][8].
The central insight is that helium II can behave as though it contains two interpenetrating components. One is called the normal component. The other is the superfluid component.
These are not two physically separated liquids sitting in different regions of a container. They are components of the same quantum fluid’s macroscopic motion.
The normal component
The normal component carries entropy and participates in ordinary viscous behavior. Its motion is associated with thermal excitations. As the temperature rises, the population of these excitations increases. The normal fraction therefore becomes more significant.
The superfluid component
The superfluid component has no entropy in the ideal two-fluid description. Under suitable conditions, it can flow without ordinary viscous dissipation. Its motion is governed by the phase coherence of the quantum fluid and the constraints on allowed excitations.
At very low temperatures, the superfluid density approaches the total liquid density. Near the lambda transition, the superfluid fraction approaches zero.
Why the two components explain different experiments
Consider a narrow channel. If the geometry and flow conditions suppress ordinary motion of the normal component, the superfluid component may still move through it.
Now consider a rotating object immersed in helium II. The normal component can exert viscous drag. The superfluid’s response is different.
Both experiments can therefore report different apparent resistance to motion without contradicting each other. The two-fluid description explains how the same liquid can support both ordinary transport and extraordinary quantum flow.
But there is a subtle point here. The two components should not be imagined as fixed sets of individual atoms. We cannot meaningfully paint some helium atoms blue and declare that those atoms are always superfluid.
The two-fluid model describes collective behavior. It is a hydrodynamic decomposition of the same interacting liquid. That distinction becomes particularly important when we discuss Bose-Einstein condensation.
8. The biggest misconception: superfluidity is not simply Bose-Einstein condensation
This is where many popular explanations of superfluid helium become misleading.
Helium-4 atoms are bosons. A helium-4 atom contains an even number of constituent fermions, giving the atom integer total spin. Bosons can occupy the same quantum state.
In an ideal Bose gas, sufficiently low temperatures produce Bose-Einstein condensation, in which a macroscopic population occupies a single quantum state.
In 1938, Fritz London suggested a connection between the lambda transition in helium and Bose-Einstein condensation [9]. That was an important theoretical step.
But liquid helium-4 is not an ideal Bose gas. It is a strongly interacting quantum liquid. Its particles remain correlated. Their collective excitations matter. And its condensate fraction is very different from its superfluid fraction.
The number that changes the explanation
In 2017, researchers reported high-resolution neutron measurements of liquid helium-4. The measurements agreed with quantum Monte Carlo calculations predicting a Bose condensate fraction of approximately 7.5% below one Kelvin [10].
Now compare that with the superfluid fraction. As temperature approaches absolute zero, the superfluid density approaches essentially the entire fluid density.
So at very low temperatures, the fluid can be almost entirely superfluid while only a small fraction of atoms occupies the single-particle Bose condensate.
That sounds contradictory only if we assume that condensate fraction and superfluid fraction are the same measurement. They are not.
Condensate fraction
This describes the macroscopic occupation of a single-particle quantum state, defined through the many-body system’s one-particle density matrix or momentum distribution.
Superfluid fraction
This describes how the fluid responds to motion, phase twists, or boundary conditions. It is connected to collective coherence and the energetic cost of inducing flow.
The two quantities are related through the physics of the interacting system. But they are not identical.
A strongly interacting quantum liquid does not require every atom to occupy the zero-momentum condensate in order to exhibit superfluidity.
This is one of the most important conceptual corrections to the original article. Superfluid helium is not merely a container in which every atom has become indistinguishable by entering one single-particle state. It is a strongly correlated many-body system with a macroscopic quantum order parameter and extraordinary collective transport behavior.
9. Phonons, rotons, and why frictionless flow is possible
The next puzzle is more subtle. Why can superfluid helium move without ordinary viscous dissipation?
It is not enough to say that the atoms are bosons. We need to understand how the liquid can gain or lose energy.
Landau approached the problem through the spectrum of elementary excitations. Instead of treating the fluid as independent atoms, he studied collective disturbances. Two important parts of that spectrum are called phonons and rotons.
Phonons: collective sound excitations
A phonon is a quantum of a collective wave. In a fluid, low-momentum phonons correspond to sound-like excitations.
Many particles participate in the disturbance. It is not simply one helium atom moving independently through the rest.
At sufficiently small momentum, the excitation energy increases approximately linearly with momentum. The proportionality is related to the speed of sound.
Rotons: an unusual finite-momentum minimum
At higher momenta, helium’s excitation spectrum becomes more complicated. One distinctive feature is a local minimum called the roton minimum.
Near this minimum, excitations have an energy scale of approximately 8.6 to 8.7 Kelvin when expressed in temperature-equivalent units. The characteristic wavenumber is approximately 1.9 inverse ångströms.
A roton is not established to be a temporary piece of crystal that forms and disappears. That image may be used as a historical analogy, but it is not an adequate modern definition. A roton is a collective excitation associated with the characteristic structure of the liquid’s excitation spectrum. Its microscopic interpretation involves correlations in the many-body fluid [8][11].
Landau’s critical-velocity argument
Imagine the superfluid moving past an obstacle. For the flow to lose energy, the system must be able to create excitations while conserving energy and momentum.
Landau showed that the excitation spectrum imposes a threshold. Below a sufficiently low relative velocity, an ideal moving fluid cannot create the relevant excitations while satisfying the conservation laws. The flow is therefore protected from that form of dissipation.
The idealized critical velocity is determined by the minimum ratio of excitation energy to momentum. In compact notation, this is often written as:
critical velocity = min[E(p) / p]
Here, E(p) is the energy of an excitation carrying momentum p.
This principle is much more informative than saying helium atoms simply stop colliding. The atoms continue interacting. Those interactions create the collective quantum state and its excitation spectrum. The suppression of dissipation emerges from the structure of the possible excitations.
What our companion lab calculates
Using a local roton approximation with published low-temperature parameters, the lab obtains an idealized Landau threshold of approximately 58.4 meters per second.
That is an instructive number. But it is not a universal speed at which every sample of helium suddenly becomes dissipative.
Real superfluid flows can become dissipative at much lower speeds because of quantized vortex nucleation, boundaries, roughness, turbulence, and other mechanisms.
The Landau criterion characterizes one important route to dissipation in an idealized flow. It does not describe every possible failure of superfluid motion.
Quantized vortices
The superfluid also has an extraordinary response to rotation.
In ordinary classical fluids, circulation can vary continuously. In a superfluid, circulation around appropriate closed paths is quantized. Vorticity becomes concentrated in quantized vortex structures.
These vortices can move, interact, reconnect, and produce complicated forms of quantum turbulence. They are a reminder that superfluidity does not mean the liquid is always perfectly calm.
A rotating superfluid can exhibit remarkably complex motion. Its complexity is simply constrained by quantum mechanics.
10. The liquid that climbs walls and produces fountains
Two of helium II’s most visually striking behaviors are the creeping film and the fountain effect. Both are real. But their explanations require more care than saying the liquid has zero friction.
The creeping film
Place superfluid helium in a container. Under suitable conditions, it can form an extremely thin film along the container’s surfaces. This is often called a Rollin film.
Helium strongly wets many solid surfaces because of their attractive interactions. The film can spread over the wall and continue across the rim.
If the geometry permits a connected flow path to a lower gravitational potential, helium may flow out of the container through the film. This can make a vessel appear to empty itself.
The process does not violate gravity. The film is not an unlimited upward-flowing liquid that gains gravitational potential energy for free.
The film’s formation depends on wetting and surface interactions. Its subsequent mass transport depends on chemical-potential and gravitational conditions. Superfluid hydrodynamics allows the connected film to carry liquid with unusually little resistance.
In May 1938, John Daunt and Kurt Mendelssohn published experiments on helium II transfer over glass surfaces. Their paper explicitly referred back to Onnes’s 1922 observations of unusual level equalization [12].
That is one of the clearest historical connections between early anomalies and the later understanding of superfluidity.
The fountain effect
A few months after the decisive flow experiments, John F. Allen and H. Jones reported another extraordinary phenomenon. Liquid helium II could produce a fountain when heat was applied to part of an apparatus containing a porous restriction.
Imagine two regions of helium II connected through a porous material. The restriction behaves as a superleak. It allows the superfluid component to pass far more easily than the normal component.
Now heat one side. The temperature difference creates a thermomechanical pressure difference. Superfluid flows toward the warmer region.
If the warmer region is connected to a narrow outlet, the resulting pressure can drive a visible jet of helium. A fountain appears.
The energy is supplied by the heater. There is no hidden violation of energy conservation. The unusual part is how temperature and pressure are coupled through the superfluid’s two-component hydrodynamics.
Allen and Jones’s 1938 paper established the effect experimentally [13]. It remains one of the most striking demonstrations of quantum-fluid behavior visible on a macroscopic scale.
11. Second sound: when heat travels as a wave
In everyday materials, heat commonly spreads through diffusion. Warm one end of a metal rod. Energy gradually moves through the material. The temperature distribution broadens over time.
But in helium II, heat transport can behave very differently.
Because the normal and superfluid components can move relative to each other, the fluid can support counterflow. The normal component transports entropy away from the heated region. The superfluid component can flow in the opposite direction.
Under suitable conditions, the net mass flow can be small even while a substantial heat flux is present. This is the basis of helium II’s remarkable heat-transport properties.
What is second sound?
Ordinary sound is primarily a wave of pressure and density.
Second sound in helium II is primarily a wave of temperature and entropy. Instead of thermal disturbances simply spreading diffusively, they can propagate as waves.
This was a prediction of two-fluid hydrodynamics. The effect was observed experimentally by Vasily Peshkov in the 1940s. Later measurements characterized the speed of second sound.
For example, a 1947 Physical Review paper reported a maximum measured second-sound speed of approximately 20.46 meters per second near 1.7 Kelvin in its experimental conditions [14].
Second sound is therefore not merely a metaphor. It is a measurable propagation mode.
Why the visible boiling can disappear
The extraordinary heat-transport behavior of helium II can suppress local thermal gradients that would otherwise produce nucleate boiling. Heat entering the liquid can be redistributed rapidly through counterflow.
That helps explain why the surface can become remarkably calm below the lambda transition under certain experimental conditions.
But saying that helium II cannot boil would be incorrect. It still evaporates. Its heat transport can also become turbulent or encounter other limits. The better statement is that its thermal behavior changes so dramatically that familiar boiling patterns can disappear.
12. Why helium refuses to freeze without pressure
One of Onnes’s early puzzles was that helium remained liquid at extraordinarily low temperatures. Most familiar substances become solid when cooled sufficiently. Why not helium?
The answer involves the competition between interatomic attraction and quantum zero-point motion.
The classical expectation
In a classical picture, cooling reduces thermal motion. At sufficiently low temperatures, attractive interactions can favor the formation of a solid crystal. The atoms become localized around lattice positions.
That is broadly what happens in many ordinary materials. But the classical picture becomes incomplete near absolute zero.
Zero-point motion
Quantum mechanics prevents particles from having simultaneously exact positions and momenta. If an atom becomes strongly localized, its momentum uncertainty increases. That contributes a minimum kinetic-energy cost to confinement.
Even at zero temperature, quantum particles do not simply become motionless classical points. This is zero-point motion.
Helium atoms are light. Their interatomic attractions are weak. The zero-point motion is therefore unusually important relative to the energy favoring a crystal lattice.
At low pressure, the quantum liquid remains more stable than a solid even as temperature approaches zero.
But helium can freeze
The original draft claimed that helium never freezes. That is incorrect without a pressure qualification.
Helium-4 can be solidified by applying sufficient pressure. At very low temperatures, the relevant pressure is roughly 25 bar, or about 25 atmospheres.
Pressure changes the balance between the liquid and solid phases. The atoms can be forced into a crystalline state despite their zero-point motion.
Helium therefore does not violate the principles of solidification. Its phase diagram is unusual because the low-pressure liquid remains stable down to extremely low temperatures. That is another direct consequence of quantum mechanics becoming important at the scale of an entire material [15].
13. The superfluid that cools the Large Hadron Collider
Superfluid helium is not only a laboratory curiosity. It is also important cryogenic infrastructure.
At CERN, the Large Hadron Collider uses superconducting magnets to guide and focus beams of high-energy particles. Those magnets require extremely low temperatures.
CERN reports that the LHC’s main magnets operate at approximately 1.9 Kelvin. That is below the helium-4 lambda transition. The coolant is therefore in the helium II regime.
CERN’s cryogenic system uses approximately 120 tonnes of helium to maintain the required operating conditions. Its infrastructure includes thousands of leak-tight connections, enormous refrigeration equipment, and a 27-kilometer accelerator ring [16].
Why use superfluid helium?
The LHC magnets operate with very large electrical currents. Superconductivity prevents ordinary electrical resistance from generating the heat that would otherwise make such currents impractical.
But superconducting magnets still require thermal stability. Any local heat disturbance must be managed carefully. Superfluid helium’s thermal properties make it particularly valuable for distributing and extracting heat.
This is a connection between two quantum phenomena that Onnes’s laboratory encountered during the same early experiments. Superconductivity allows the magnets to carry enormous currents. Superfluid helium helps maintain the temperatures needed for that superconductivity.
The two discoveries eventually became partners in one of the largest scientific machines ever built.
There is something elegant about that history. The liquid that Onnes first produced in a quantity small enough for a cup eventually became part of an industrial cryogenic system operating around the world’s most powerful particle accelerator.
14. From liquid helium to laboratory black-hole analogues
Modern superfluid physics reaches far beyond cryogenics. One particularly fascinating direction uses fluid motion to simulate aspects of curved spacetime.
The basic idea comes from analogue gravity. In general relativity, spacetime curvature influences how fields and waves propagate. Under certain conditions, small waves moving through a flowing fluid can obey equations mathematically analogous to fields propagating in an effective curved spacetime.
This does not turn the fluid into an actual gravitational black hole. It creates a physical system in which some wave-propagation phenomena have a useful mathematical analogy to gravity.
The Nottingham quantum vortex
In March 2024, researchers led by Patrik Švančara and collaborators, including Silke Weinfurtner at the University of Nottingham, published a Nature paper titled Rotating curved spacetime signatures from a giant quantum vortex [17].
The team stabilized an unusually large vortex in superfluid helium-4. The vortex carried thousands of circulation quanta.
The researchers studied waves interacting with the vortex flow. They observed bound states and signatures analogous to the ringdown behavior associated with rotating black-hole systems.
That is a significant result. The experiment created a controllable analogue system for investigating wave dynamics resembling certain aspects of curved spacetime.
What the experiment did not prove
It did not create an astrophysical black hole. It did not directly reproduce real gravitational spacetime curvature. And the specific 2024 experiment should not be described as a direct observation of Hawking radiation.
The published result concerns a giant quantum vortex and analogue rotating-spacetime signatures, including ringdown behavior.
Analogue gravity experiments can illuminate theoretical relationships. They do not automatically establish that every phenomenon in the analogue has occurred in an astrophysical system.
That distinction makes the research more scientifically meaningful, not less. A well-defined analogy is useful precisely because we know which aspects are shared and which are not.
The remarkable continuity
Onnes built a laboratory to investigate matter at very low temperatures. He discovered that familiar substances could behave in unexpected ways.
More than a century later, researchers use related quantum-fluid physics to explore collective excitations, quantum vortices, and mathematical analogies to gravity.
The same material remains an experimental doorway into concepts that are difficult to study directly.

The second figure compares two low-temperature observables.
The superfluid density fraction approaches 100% as temperature approaches zero. The Bose condensate fraction remains approximately 7.5% below one Kelvin, according to the cited neutron-scattering research.
The values describe different physical quantities. The chart is not a temperature-dependent fit or an experimental reproduction. Its purpose is to correct the common misconception that the entire superfluid must be a single-particle Bose condensate.
Experiment 3: phonons and rotons
The third experiment implements simple, literature-based approximations.
For small momenta, the phonon energy grows approximately linearly with momentum. Near the roton minimum, a local parabolic approximation describes the excitation energy.
The program plots these two regimes. It intentionally leaves the intermediate region unspecified rather than fabricating a complete measured dispersion curve.
This is important. A simplified model should not imply greater accuracy than its assumptions support.
Experiment 4: Landau’s critical velocity
The fourth experiment uses the roton approximation to calculate the idealized flow speed at which a corresponding excitation can become energetically allowed.
The result is approximately 58.4 meters per second.
The plot then demonstrates what happens to the excitation energy in a moving frame as the flow speed increases. Below the idealized threshold, the modeled excitation remains energetically costly. At the threshold, the energy can approach zero. Above it, the model permits negative-energy excitations relative to the moving flow.
That illustrates the physical reasoning behind Landau’s critical-velocity criterion. It does not predict the onset of dissipation in an actual vessel. Real flows can develop vortices and turbulence at much lower speeds.

What the lab does and doesn’t do
It demonstrates source-based approximations for selected properties of helium-4. It does not simulate a complete many-body quantum liquid. It does not predict the lambda transition from first principles. It does not reproduce the full measured phonon-roton spectrum. It does not simulate a real fountain or creeping film. And it does not claim to measure superfluidity.
Those limitations are explicit because a useful scientific tutorial should distinguish experimentally established facts, theoretical approximations, and pedagogical models. The goal is to make the physical concepts easier to understand and investigate.
16. What Onnes’s missed discovery teaches us
Onnes died on February 21, 1926. He was 72 years old.
He had transformed low-temperature physics. He had built a laboratory capable of reaching temperatures no one else had reliably achieved. He had liquefied helium. He had discovered superconductivity. And he had recorded observations that would later become part of the history of superfluidity.
But the recognition of superfluidity required more than repeating his original experiments.
It required new flow measurements. It required new thermodynamic interpretations. It required Bose-Einstein statistics. It required a collective description of quantum fluids. And it required scientists willing to question whether ordinary hydrodynamics was sufficient.
Was it really an example of missing the obvious?
From our modern perspective, liquid helium stopping its familiar boiling behavior is a dramatic clue. But the significance becomes obvious partly because we already know the answer.
Scientific observations do not arrive labeled with the theory that explains them. A strange data point may indicate an instrument problem. An unexpected temperature change may come from an unstable control system. An apparent anomaly may reflect contamination. All of those explanations must be considered.
Good science does not involve treating every unusual measurement as a revolution. It involves designing experiments that distinguish ordinary explanations from genuinely new phenomena.
That is exactly what the 1938 experiments achieved. They did not merely repeat the claim that helium II was strange. They measured behavior that ordinary viscous-flow theory could not account for.
The experiments changed the status of the question. The phenomenon was no longer an unexplained oddity. It demanded a different physical theory.
A more useful lesson than the heroic-discovery story
The history is often told as a story about a brilliant physicist overlooking something extraordinary. That framing is memorable. But it underestimates the collective nature of the discovery.
Onnes and his Leiden technicians made the relevant temperatures accessible. Later researchers measured density, heat capacity, thermal conductivity, and unusual mass transport. Kapitsa, Allen, and Misener established the striking flow behavior. London connected the transition with quantum statistics. Tisza and Landau developed the theoretical language needed to describe the new fluid. Others measured second sound, studied creeping films, and explored quantized vortices.
The understanding of superfluidity was not the product of one person finally seeing what everyone else had missed. It emerged from a sequence of experiments and theories that gradually made the phenomenon recognizable.
That history is a better picture of how science actually works.
Conclusion: the liquid was already there. The understanding wasn’t.
On July 10, 1908, Onnes produced roughly sixty milliliters of liquid helium. In the same historic experiment, he cooled it into a temperature range where the helium was already superfluid.
In April 1911, while discovering superconductivity, he recorded the sudden disappearance of visible boiling as the helium crossed the lambda transition.
In the following decades, more evidence accumulated. Then, in January 1938, two independent experimental papers established the extraordinary flow behavior that came to define superfluidity.
The discoveries were separated by almost thirty years. Not because helium refused to reveal its properties. But because producing a physical phenomenon and understanding its nature are different scientific achievements.
Today, we know why helium-4 is so unusual. Its light atoms and weak interactions make quantum zero-point motion important enough to prevent solidification at low pressure. Its many-body quantum state supports a superfluid component with extraordinary transport properties. Its excitation spectrum constrains when moving flow can dissipate energy. Its quantized vortices reveal quantum mechanics on a scale visible in macroscopic fluid motion. Its thermal properties make it useful for cooling some of the most demanding scientific machines in the world. And its collective waves can even provide laboratory analogues of certain curved-spacetime phenomena.
All of that physics was waiting in a liquid that Onnes first held in a quantity small enough to fit inside a teacup.
The lesson is not that brilliant scientists inevitably overlook obvious discoveries. It is that the world does not have to match the concepts we currently use to describe it. The data can be real. The measurements can be accurate. The apparatus can work exactly as intended. And the meaning can still remain beyond the reach of the existing theory.
Sometimes the most important experiment is not the one that produces a new phenomenon. It is the one that finally teaches us what we have been looking at all along.
